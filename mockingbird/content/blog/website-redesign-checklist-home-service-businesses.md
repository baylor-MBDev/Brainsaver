Most home service websites don't need more pages. They need to load fast on a phone, say clearly what you do and where you do it, and keep a call or quote button within reach of a thumb. This website redesign checklist walks through those basics in the order we'd tackle them, so you can judge your current site, brief whoever builds the new one, and check their work after launch.

It's the same list of things we look at when we review a service business's website. You don't need to be technical to use it.

## Measure before you start

A redesign should fix specific problems, so write those problems down first. Otherwise you'll end up with a site that looks newer and performs the same.

- **Run Google PageSpeed Insights on mobile.** Paste your homepage into [pagespeed.web.dev](https://pagespeed.web.dev) and note the performance score and the "Largest Contentful Paint" time. That's how long the main content takes to show up on a phone.
- **Check Google Search Console.** If it's set up, export the search queries and pages that already bring you visitors. If it isn't, set it up now; it's free, and it's the only way to see which searches you show up for.
- **List where leads come from today.** Phone calls, the contact form, your Google Business Profile, referrals. If you don't know, start asking every new customer how they found you.
- **Save your list of pages that get traffic.** If any of those URLs change in the redesign, each one needs a redirect to its new address (more on that below).

## The phone test

Most people looking for a plumber or an HVAC tech are on their phone, often in a hurry. Open your site on your own phone and check:

- Does the layout fit the screen, or do you have to pinch and zoom?
- Is your phone number in the header, and does tapping it start a call?
- Is there a call or quote button visible without scrolling? A button that stays fixed at the bottom of the screen works well on phones.
- Can you read the text without zooming? Body text under 16px is hard to read on a phone.
- Are buttons and links big enough to tap without hitting the wrong one?

If the site fails the first question, it isn't built for phones at all, and a rebuild is usually less work than patching it.

## Speed

Slow sites lose visitors before they ever see your offer, and Google uses page experience as one of its ranking signals. The usual culprits on small business sites are easy to spot:

- **Huge images.** A photo straight off a phone can be several megabytes. Images should be resized for the web and served in modern formats like WebP or AVIF.
- **Too many plugins and scripts.** Every chat widget, slider, tracking pixel, and page-builder add-on adds weight. Keep the ones that earn their place.
- **Hosting.** Cheap shared hosting can make every page wait on the server before anything loads. A content delivery network (CDN) serves your pages from locations close to each visitor.
- **Autoplay video and image sliders.** They're heavy, and visitors mostly ignore them. A single strong photo of your team or your work does more.

Google's Core Web Vitals give you three numbers to aim for: Largest Contentful Paint (how fast the main content appears), Cumulative Layout Shift (whether things jump around while loading), and Interaction to Next Paint (how quickly the page responds when someone taps). PageSpeed Insights shows all three.

## Make the next step obvious

Every page should have one main action, and visitors shouldn't have to look for it.

- **Pick the action per page.** On an emergency service page it's "Call now." On a remodeling page it might be "Request an estimate."
- **Put it above the fold** and repeat it at the end of the page.
- **Keep forms short.** Name, phone, and "what do you need help with?" is enough for a first contact. Every extra field costs you submissions.
- **Confirm and follow up fast.** After someone submits a form, show a clear confirmation and say when you'll get back to them. Then actually respond quickly; the business that answers first often gets the job.

## Trust signals customers look for

People are letting you into their home. Your site should show them who you are.

- **Real photos** of your team, trucks, and finished work. Stock photos of smiling models read as generic.
- **License and insurance details**, if your trade is licensed in your state. Some states also have rules about displaying license numbers in advertising, so check yours.
- **Reviews.** Link to your Google reviews or embed them with a widget. Never write or edit reviews yourself.
- **Your service area**, stated plainly: the cities and neighborhoods you actually serve.
- **How pricing works.** You don't have to publish prices, but explaining things like trip fees, free estimates, or financing removes a reason to hesitate.

## Local SEO foundations

A redesign is the right moment to fix the structure Google uses to understand your business.

- **One page per core service.** "Water heater repair" and "drain cleaning" each deserve their own page with real detail, not one page that lists everything.
- **Service area pages only where you genuinely work.** A page for each nearby city can help, but dozens of near-identical pages with the city name swapped in can hurt more than help. Each one should say something specific to that area.
- **Consistent business details.** Your business name, address, and phone number should match your Google Business Profile exactly.
- **Business schema markup.** Structured data (LocalBusiness or a more specific type like Plumber) tells search engines your hours, service area, and contact details in a format they read directly.
- **Clear page titles.** Each page title should say what the page is and where, like "Water Heater Repair in Waco, TX | Your Company."

## Technical housekeeping

These are the details that quietly break during redesigns.

- **HTTPS everywhere**, with the plain http:// version redirecting to the secure one. Browsers label non-HTTPS sites "Not secure."
- **301 redirects** from every old URL that changed to its new address, so you keep the search rankings and links those pages earned.
- **An XML sitemap and a robots.txt file**, submitted in Search Console after launch.
- **Analytics that work.** If your site still runs Universal Analytics, it stopped collecting data in July 2023. Set up Google Analytics 4 or a privacy-friendly alternative.
- **Call tracking** if you advertise, so you know which channels make the phone ring.
- **Accessibility basics**: descriptive alt text on images, enough color contrast, and a site that works with a keyboard. It's the right thing to do, and it overlaps heavily with good SEO.
- **Fresh content.** Remove old promotions, update the copyright year, and make sure your hours are current. A footer that says "© 2019" makes a busy business look closed.

## After launch

The first few weeks after launch are when problems surface.

- Submit the new sitemap in Search Console and watch for "page not found" errors.
- Run PageSpeed Insights again and compare with your baseline.
- Submit your own contact form once a week to make sure messages still arrive.
- Keep adding real content: photos from recent jobs, answers to questions customers ask on the phone, and new service pages as you add services.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does a website redesign take for a small service business?

It depends mostly on how many pages you need and how ready your content is. A focused site with a handful of service pages can come together in a few weeks. The most common delay isn't the build; it's gathering photos, reviews, and details about your services.

### Will a redesign hurt my Google rankings?

It can if pages that rank today disappear or move without redirects. Keep the content that already performs, redirect every changed URL to its new address, and submit your sitemap after launch. Done carefully, a faster, better-structured site usually helps rankings over time.

### Do I need a new website or just a refresh?

If your current site is fast, works well on phones, and you can edit it easily, a refresh of the design and content may be enough. If it fails the phone test, scores poorly on PageSpeed, or you can't update it without calling someone, a rebuild is usually the better investment.

### Should I stay on Wix or Squarespace?

Website builders are a reasonable choice for many small businesses, especially early on. It's worth moving to a custom site when speed, SEO control, or owning your site outright start to matter more than the convenience of a drag-and-drop editor.
