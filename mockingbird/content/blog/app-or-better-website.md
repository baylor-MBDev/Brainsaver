You need an app when customers will use it again and again, and it does something a website can't do as well, like sending timely notifications, using the phone's camera or location, or making a logged-in routine (booking a class, reordering, checking rewards) take two taps instead of ten. If customers interact with you a few times a year, a better website will almost always serve them, and you, better.

If you're asking "do I need an app for my business?", the rest of this post gives you a quick test, the honest tradeoffs, the middle options, and a checklist you can run through in five minutes.

## The repeat-use test

An app lives or dies on whether people keep it on their phone. Before anything else, ask:

- **How often will a typical customer use it?** Weekly or more is app territory. Monthly is borderline. A few times a year almost never justifies an install.
- **Is there a routine?** Booking the same class every Tuesday, reordering supplies, scanning a membership card at the door, tracking a loyalty balance. Routines are what apps do best.
- **Would customers want notifications from you?** "Your table is ready," "class starts in an hour," and "your order shipped" are welcome. "Check out our blog" isn't.

If you answered "rarely, no, and no," stop here and put the budget into your website.

## What apps do better than websites

- **Notifications that people actually see**, delivered to the lock screen.
- **A permanent spot on the home screen**, which makes repeat visits nearly effortless.
- **Device features**: the camera, location, Bluetooth, health data, and working offline. Our own app, DOOMTYPE, counts pushups through the phone's camera using on-device pose detection, which is the kind of thing a website can't do well.
- **Smoother logged-in experiences.** Staying signed in, saved payment methods, and fast navigation between screens.

## What websites do better

- **Being found.** People discover businesses through Google and AI search, and they land on websites, not app store listings.
- **No install.** A link works instantly for anyone, on any device. Every install step loses people.
- **Lower cost to build and change.** One website versus two app store releases, each going through review.
- **Sharing.** A website page can be texted, emailed, or posted anywhere.

For most businesses, the website is the front door and an app is the membership card. You need a good front door first. If yours needs work, start with our [website redesign checklist](/blog/website-redesign-checklist-home-service-businesses/).

## The middle options

It isn't a binary choice. Three in-between options are worth knowing about:

- **A better mobile website with accounts.** Many "we need an app" requests are really "customers need to log in and do X from their phone." A fast, well-built mobile site can do that.
- **A progressive web app (PWA).** A website that can be installed to the home screen, work partly offline, and, on iPhone since iOS 16.4, send push notifications once added to the home screen. It's a good fit when you want app-like convenience without the app stores.
- **The branded app your software vendor offers.** Booking and point-of-sale platforms for gyms, salons, and restaurants often sell a white-label app. It's quick to launch, but you're limited to the features and design the vendor supports, and customers may end up inside a generic marketplace.

## Costs and ongoing work

The build is only part of the cost of an app. Plan for:

- **Design and development.** Much of the cost depends on how many screens and features you need and what the app connects to (your booking system, payments, a customer database).
- **iPhone and Android.** Cross-platform frameworks like React Native let one codebase serve both, which usually lowers cost compared with building two separate native apps.
- **A backend.** Most apps need a server and database for accounts, data, and notifications.
- **App store accounts.** Apple's Developer Program costs $99 per year, and a Google Play developer account has a one-time $25 registration fee.
- **Reviews and compliance.** Both stores review every release, require a privacy policy, and ask you to disclose what data you collect (Apple's privacy labels and Google Play's Data safety form).
- **Maintenance.** New iOS and Android versions arrive every year, and apps need updates to keep working well. Budget for ongoing work, not just the launch.

## A quick decision checklist

| Question | Leans app | Leans website |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How often do customers interact with you? | Weekly or more | A few times a year |
| Is there a logged-in routine? | Yes, a clear one | Not really |
| Would notifications be welcome? | Yes, they're timely and useful | Mostly promotional |
| Do you need the camera, GPS, or offline mode? | Yes | No |
| How do new customers find you? | Mostly existing customers | Search, ads, referrals |
| Budget for ongoing updates? | Yes | Tight |

Mostly left column? An app is worth scoping. Mostly right? Invest in the website, and revisit an app when the routine is there.

## If you do build an app

Start with one core job and do it extremely well: booking, reordering, or check-in. Measure whether people come back after the first week before adding features. Plan your notifications so they're useful rather than noisy, and give the store listing and privacy forms real attention, since they're the first thing reviewers and customers see.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I just turn my website into an app?

Tools exist that wrap a website in an app shell, but Apple's App Store guidelines on minimum functionality can reject apps that are essentially a repackaged website. If you want an installable version of your site without the stores, a progressive web app is the cleaner route.

### How long does it take to build an app?

It depends on scope: the number of screens, what the app connects to, and how much design work it needs. A focused first version with one core job is much faster than a full-featured app, which is a good reason to start small and grow it based on how customers use it.

### Do I need separate iPhone and Android apps?

Not necessarily. Cross-platform frameworks such as React Native let one codebase run on both, which keeps cost and maintenance down. Fully native apps still make sense for some performance-heavy or deeply device-specific features.

### What's the difference between an app and a progressive web app?

A progressive web app is a website that can be installed to the home screen and behave more like an app, including offline use and notifications on supported devices. It doesn't go through the app stores, which is simpler and cheaper, but it can't do everything a native app can.
